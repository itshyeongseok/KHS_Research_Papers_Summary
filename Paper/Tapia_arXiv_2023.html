A Comparison between Frame-based and Event-based Cameras for Flapping-Wing Robot Perception
Tier 0 — Core
R. Tapia, J. P. Rodríguez-Gómez, J. A. Sanchez-Diaz, F. J. Gañán, I. G. Rodríguez, J. Luna-Santamaria, J. R. Martínez-de Dios, A. Ollero
2023 IEEE/RSJ International Conference on Intelligent Robots and Systems (IROS), Detroit, USA, October 1–5, 2023, pp. 3025–3032 · DOI: 10.1109/IROS55552.2023.10341770 · arXiv:2309.05450
Paper Summary
Tapia et al. perform the first experimental head-to-head between frame-based and event-based cameras onboard an ornithopter (the E-Flap, Zufferey 2021), comparing seven cameras (DAVIS346 DVS+APS, RealSense D435, ZED, eCapture G53, ELP Mini-720p, mvBlueFOX-C/G) along three requirement axes: platform constraints (weight, size), flight requirements (power, dynamic range, motion blur), and application performance (corner detection, VIO, line detection, human detection). Across three test benches that emulate flapping pitch (±30°, 0–6 Hz) and a flight campaign at mean flapping rate 4.31 Hz / 3.06 m/s, the DVS event sensor outperforms frame cameras on power, dynamic range (>83 dB before degradation), and motion-blur robustness; in flight, VINS-MONO on APS frames reports the lowest VIO error (E_RMS 0.828 m) but Ultimate SLAM on event images (E_RMS 0.879 m) and event-based corner detection (eHarris* F1 0.900) match frame performance. The conclusion frames event cameras as the most promising sensor for ornithopters while flagging open issues — onboard processing bottlenecks (~0.97 M events/s vs. 0.22 M event corner-detector throughput) and the immature event-vision software stack.

Introduction (click for detail)
The introduction frames ornithopters as bioinspired aerial platforms with combined flapping/gliding modes that face severe perception constraints (payload, weight distribution, energy) and flight stresses (motion blur, abrupt lighting changes from pitch swings). It positions cameras as the de facto sensor (LiDAR/radar/IR/ultrasound being unsuitable), notes that prior works variably picked frame or event cameras, and presents the paper's contribution: the first onboard experimental head-to-head between frame and event cameras for ornithopter perception, evaluated on seven cameras across requirement axes and a flight campaign.
Related Works (click for detail)
Section II surveys ornithopter perception history (motion-capture-based control, mechanical camera stabilizers, lightweight off-board video, GRIFFIN dataset, event-based onboard guidance + sense-and-avoid) and theoretical/empirical event-vs-frame camera comparisons in non-FWAV settings. The takeaway: prior FWAV perception is split across sensor families with no consensus, and prior comparisons are either theoretical or off-FWAV; this paper closes the experimental ornithopter gap.
Method (click for detail)
Section III–V develop the comparison methodology. Three requirement classes are set up — ornithopter platform requirements (weight, size), ornithopter flight requirements (power, dynamic range, motion blur), application-dependent requirements (corner, VIO, line, human detection performance). Seven cameras (Table I) span the practical FWAV sensor space. Three test benches (Figure 2) emulate flapping pitch motion (±30°, linear ±2.5 cm), oscillating motion for blur, and dynamic-range ArUco detection; the application bench is the E-Flap ornithopter in flight at 4.31 Hz mean flap rate.
Experiment (click for detail)
Sections IV–VI present the test-bench and flight results. DVS (event) leads frame cameras on power consumption (lower than most frame cameras across 1–6 Hz), dynamic range (~100% ArUco detection up to ~83 dB vs APS ~45 dB), and motion-blur robustness (thickness ratio ~1 across pitch rates). On the ornithopter, application performance is more nuanced: APS frames lead on corner Precision (0.972), VIO RMSE (VINS-MONO+APS 0.828 m), line Prevalence (0.943), and human detection Accuracy (0.960); event-based methods are competitive (eHarris* F1 0.900, Ultimate SLAM 0.879 m), and RS+VINS-MONO underperforms on motion-blur-affected RGB images.
Discussion (click for detail)
Section VII reasons through the question ‘which sensor is more suitable for flapping-wing robots?’. Power, motion blur, dynamic range, and weight/size all favor event cameras; in-flight application benchmarks favor APS frames slightly, but the gap is small and event-based representations sometimes lead. The authors hedge with two caveats — the software-stack maturity gap favoring frame cameras and the open question of onboard processing throughput — but conclude event cameras are the most promising sensor family for ornithopters.
Conclusion (click for detail)
Section VIII restates the contribution as the first onboard ornithopter event-vs-frame camera comparison, concludes that event cameras are the most promising vision sensor family for ornithopters, and identifies the future-work agenda: extend the comparison to additional frame and high-resolution event cameras, and analyze the onboard processing constraints on lightweight ornithopter computers.
